Research Vision

How much of a material's deformation is stored, and how much is gone for good?

Soft materials store and dissipate deformation at the same time. A paste, a gel or a polymer network springs back from a small deformation and flows under a larger one, and in most real processing it is doing both at once. Conventional rheometry measures that combined response precisely, and most of what we know about these materials rests on it. What a bulk measurement does not resolve is how much of the deformation the material kept.

Recovery rheology answers that question directly, and it does so by building on the same oscillatory measurements rather than replacing them. Tracking how much deformation a material gives back once the stress is removed separates the recoverable part from the unrecoverable. Two materials with the same modulus can divide it quite differently, and that difference is often what determines how each one behaves in a process.

We treat recoverable and unrecoverable deformation as two measurable quantities, not one.

I have put this to work across materials that behave very differently from one another: particle-filled inks and printable pastes, colloidal gels and glasses, associative polymer networks, protein gels, and particle-laden interfaces. In printable pastes, where the two components sit relative to one another is what decides whether an ink can be extruded and still hold its shape. In yield-stress fluids, separating them showed that yielding is a continuous transition rather than a switch between solid and liquid behavior, which led to a constitutive description of yielding in systems with attractive interactions between particles.

Pairing these measurements with scattering has been the most productive thread. Using x-ray scattering during shear, I helped show that the point at which a material's microstructure most closely returns to its rest configuration is the point where recoverable strain is smallest, tying a bulk mechanical quantity to structural memory at the nanometre scale. My postdoctoral work extended that pairing to neutron scattering on protein gels, and to an account of why independent ways of tracking a curing reaction can disagree in filled materials.

Ultimately, we seek to establish how far recoverable strain serves as a common description across material classes.

Where the program goes:

My group will extend recovery measurements in three directions, each asking the same question in a setting where a single bulk number leaves something unresolved.

On command

Some soft materials can be stiffened, softened or restructured by an applied external field, whether during processing or in use. We ask how a field shifts the balance between what a material stores and what it gives up permanently, rather than only how it changes the overall stiffness.

In space

Real flows are rarely even. Different regions of one sample can experience different histories, and a bulk measurement averages across all of them. We want measurements that resolve stored and permanent deformation locally, and relate them to how the material is organized in those regions.

In time

Measuring what a material has stored normally means stopping the flow and letting it relax, which changes the very history being studied and is not an option in a process that has to keep running. We want measurements that follow stored deformation continuously, while the deformation is still going on.

How we work:

Oscillatory and rotational rheometry, recovery rheology, constitutive modeling, and x-ray and neutron scattering, used together so that a mechanical measurement and a structural one describe the same sample under the same deformation history.
