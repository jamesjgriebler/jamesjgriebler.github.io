Research Vision

We can measure the deformation a material stores. Why only in bulk, and only after the flow has stopped?

The idea that strain separates into a recoverable part and an unrecoverable part is as old as the first viscoelastic models, and over the last decade it has been shown that the separation can be measured directly, and that it accounts for a great deal that a single bulk measurement leaves unexplained. I helped develop these techniques in graduate school, and it has informed the questions I want to explore

The measurement is simple enough. Remove the stress, and the material hands back part of the strain. That part was stored, and it is the recoverable strain; what stays behind is unrecoverable. I have used recovery rheology on particle-filled inks and printable inks, colloidal gels and glasses, associative polymer networks, protein gels, and yield stress fluids. In printable pastes, where the two strains sit relative to one another is what decides whether an ink can be extruded and still hold its shape. In yield stress fluids, separating them showed that yielding is a continuous transition rather than a switch between solid and liquid behavior, hich led to a constitutive description of yielding in systems with attractive interactions between particles.

Scattering is how I connect the bulk measurement to what the material is actually doing. Using x-ray scattering during shear, I helped show that the point at which a microstructure most closely returns to its rest configuration is also the point where recoverable strain is smallest, tying a bulk mechanical quantity to structural memory at the micro-scale. My postdoctoral work extended that pairing to neutron scattering on protein gels, and to an account of why independent ways of tracking a curing reaction can disagree in filled materials.

The next chapter is looking inside the material to develop a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms. Can we control the amount of stored deformatikon through the applications of external fields? Can we resolve the stored and permanent deformation in space for materials which flow heterogeneously? Can we measure the stored deformation while the material is still flowing?

Those three questions drive my research:

On command

External fields can change the microstructure of soft materials, either during processing or while in use. Bulk rheometric signals report changes in the composite response, but the underlying balance between what the material stores and dissipates is critical to processing and controlling this response. Once developed, these techinques will unlock advancements in vibration dampeners and actuators, active robitcs, and controllable fluids

In space

Real flows are rarely even. Different regions of a sample experience different histories, and a bulk measurement averages across all of them. We are working toward measurements that resolve stored and permanent deformation locally, and relate them to how the microstructure is organized in those regions.

In time

Measuring what a material has stored normally means stopping the flow and letting it recover, which changes the very history being studied and is not an option in a process that has to keep running. We are working on ways to follow stored deformation while the deformation is still going on.

How we work:

Oscillatory and rotational rheometry, recovery rheology, constitutive modeling, and x-ray and neutron scattering, run together so that the mechanical measurement and the structural one come from the same sample under the same deformation history.
