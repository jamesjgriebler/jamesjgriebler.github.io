Research Vision

How much of a material's deformation is stored, and how much is gone for good?

Soft materials sit between solid and liquid. A paste, a gel, a suspension or a printing ink will store deformation and spring back when pushed gently, then flow and stay where it is put when pushed harder. Both behaviors happen at once, and both matter to anyone who has to process these materials. Yet rheometry reports on the material as a whole, returning a single number in which the two are mixed together.

Recovery rheology separates them. By measuring how much deformation a material gives back once the stress is removed, it divides what was stored from what was permanent. That division changes the conclusions. Applied to yield-stress fluids, it shows that they accumulate permanent deformation below their yield stress: materials commonly treated as solids are in fact flowing, slowly and irreversibly.

We treat stored and permanent deformation as two separate quantities, not one.

My doctoral work developed a way to make that separation without interrupting an imposed oscillation, showing that yielding is a continuous transition rather than a switch between solid and liquid behavior. It went on to produce a constitutive description of yielding in systems with attractive interactions between particles. Using x-ray scattering during shear, I helped show that the point at which a material's microstructure most closely returns to its rest configuration is the point where recoverable strain is smallest, tying a bulk mechanical quantity to structural memory at the nanometre scale.

My postdoctoral work has added structure and chemistry to that foundation: neutron scattering on protein gels, and an account of why independent ways of tracking a curing reaction disagree with one another in filled materials. That work sharpened my interest in how conditions that vary from place to place within a sample complicate the interpretation of measurements that average over it.

Ultimately, we seek to establish how far a single recoverable-strain framework can describe the behavior of complex fluids.

Where the program goes:

My group will extend recovery measurements in three directions. Each asks the same underlying question under conditions where one number for the whole sample is not enough.

On command

Some soft materials can be stiffened, softened or restructured by an applied external field, whether during processing or in use. We ask how a field shifts the balance between what a material stores and what it gives up permanently, rather than only how it changes the overall stiffness.

In space

Real flows are rarely even. Different regions of one sample can experience different histories, and a bulk measurement averages across all of them. We want measurements that resolve stored and permanent deformation locally, and relate them to how the material is organized in those regions.

In time

Measuring what a material has stored normally means stopping the flow and letting it relax, which changes the very history being studied and is not an option in a process that has to keep running. We want measurements that follow stored deformation continuously, while the deformation is still going on.

How we work:

Oscillatory and rotational rheometry, recovery rheology, constitutive modeling, and x-ray and neutron scattering, used together so that a mechanical measurement and a structural one describe the same sample under the same deformation history.
