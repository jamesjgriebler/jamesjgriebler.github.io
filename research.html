Research Vision

Soft materials sit between solid and liquid. Pushed gently, a paste or a gel stores deformation and springs back. Pushed harder, it flows and stays where it is put. Most rheological measurements report a single number for the whole sample, with those two behaviors mixed together.

My work separates them. Recovery rheology measures how much deformation a material gives back once the stress is removed, dividing what was stored from what was permanent. That division changes what we conclude about when a material yields, what it remembers of what was done to it, and how it ought to be processed.

A material usually treated as a solid can be flowing — slowly, and irreversibly — well below the stress at which it is said to yield.

What I have shown

During my doctoral work I developed a way to separate recoverable from unrecoverable deformation without interrupting an imposed oscillation. Applied to yield-stress fluids, it showed that they accumulate permanent deformation below their yield stress: yielding is a continuous transition rather than a switch between solid and liquid behavior. I went on to build a constitutive description of yielding for systems with attractive interactions between particles.

Using x-ray scattering during shear, I helped show that the point at which a material’s microstructure most closely returns to its rest configuration is the point where recoverable strain is smallest — tying a bulk mechanical quantity to structural memory at the nanometre scale.

As a postdoc I have worked on the structure of protein gels using neutron scattering, and on why independent ways of tracking a curing reaction disagree with one another in filled materials. That work sharpened my interest in how conditions varying from place to place within a sample complicate the interpretation of measurements that average over it.

Where the program goes

My group will extend recovery measurements in three directions. Each asks the same underlying question — how much deformation is stored, and how much is kept — under conditions where one number for the whole sample is not enough.

On command

Some soft materials can be stiffened, softened or restructured by an applied external field, whether during processing or in use. I am interested in how a field shifts the balance between what a material stores and what it gives up permanently, rather than only in how it changes the overall stiffness.

In space

Real flows are rarely even. Different regions of one sample can experience different histories, and a bulk measurement averages across all of them. I want measurements that resolve stored and permanent deformation locally, and relate them to how the material is organized in those regions.

In time

Measuring what a material has stored normally means stopping the flow and letting it relax — which changes the very history being studied, and is not an option in a process that has to keep running. I am interested in measurements that follow stored deformation continuously, while the deformation is still going on.

Approach

Oscillatory and rotational rheometry, recovery rheology, constitutive modeling, and x-ray and neutron scattering — used together, so that a mechanical measurement and a structural one describe the same sample under the same deformation history.
